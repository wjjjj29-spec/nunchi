How to see a doctor in Korea as a tourist

You can walk into almost any neighbourhood clinic, be seen within the hour, and pay less than a taxi from the airport. Here's how it works.

3 min readChecked 11 October 2026
At a glance1 / 6
The short version
  • Walk in. Neighbourhood clinics (의원) see tourists without an appointment. Bring your passport.
  • Cost: tourists can't use Korean health insurance, so you pay in full. The standard first-visit fee is ₩18,840 in 2026. With medicine, expect roughly ₩20,000–40,000.
  • Medicine: the clinic gives you a prescription and you buy the medicine at a pharmacy (약국) nearby.
  • Language: call 1330 for a free interpreter (7am–midnight). Emergencies: 119.

1. Find a clinic, not a hospital

For a cold, a stomach bug, an ear infection or a sprained ankle, go to a neighbourhood clinic, called a 의원 (ui-won). They are on almost every street above shops and cafés. They take walk-ins, the wait is short, and they cost a fraction of a big hospital.

Clinics specialise, so pick by what's wrong. Search the Korean word on Naver Map or Kakao Map and choose one that's open now.

University hospitals will also see you without a referral, but uninsured fees there are much higher. Save them for something serious.

2. At the front desk

Hand over your passport and say what's wrong. Pointing at the body part works. You'll fill in a short form, wait (often 10–30 minutes), see the doctor for a few minutes, and pay at the desk on the way out. Most clinics open on weekdays and Saturday mornings and close for lunch around 1–2pm.

What it costs

Short-term visitors can't join Korea's national health insurance, so you pay the full, uninsured price. That is still cheap by most countries' standards. The official first-visit consultation fee at a clinic is ₩18,840 in 2026. Clinics can charge visitors more, and tests or injections are extra.

One real example: a traveller on Reddit in October 2026 paid ₩18,800 for the visit and ₩18,500 for 15 days of blood-pressure medicine. An emergency room is a different story. Expect roughly ₩150,000–500,000 uninsured.

3. The pharmacy step

Korean clinics don't hand out medicine. You get a printed prescription, walk to a pharmacy (약국, look for the green cross, usually right downstairs) and pay there. Pharmacies also sell everyday medicine without a prescription. Just describe your symptoms.

At night, at weekends, on holidays

  • Convenience stores open 24 hours sell a short list of approved medicines: fever and pain relief, cold medicine, digestive aids and pain patches. One day's dose per pack.
  • Late-night and holiday pharmacies are designated by each city. Search open hospitals and pharmacies on E-Gen, the national emergency medical information site (Korean, but the map works).
  • Emergencies: call 119. The ambulance is free; the hospital bills you separately.

Getting help in English

  • 1330 Korea Travel Hotline: free, 7am to midnight, in English and seven other languages. They can join a three-way call with the clinic or with emergency services. From a foreign phone, dial +82-2-1330.
  • Danuri 1577-1366: 24-hour interpretation when 1330 is closed.
  • International clinics at big Seoul hospitals have English-speaking staff and handle insurance paperwork. They cost more and usually need booking. Two to know: Severance International Health Care Center in Sinchon (02-2228-5800) and Seoul National University Hospital's International Healthcare Center (02-2072-2890).
Nunchi tip

Bring your medicine packets or a photo of the label. Korean pharmacists are quick to match a foreign brand to the local equivalent, and it saves a lot of miming.

For your travel insurance claim

Before you leave the desk, ask for the receipt (영수증) and the itemised bill (진료비 세부내역서), which is usually free. If your insurer wants a diagnosis, ask for an English medical certificate. By law it costs no more than ₩20,000. Keep the pharmacy receipt too.

Questions people ask

Can tourists see a doctor in Korea without insurance?

Yes. Neighbourhood clinics see walk-in tourists. You pay the full uninsured price, which for a simple visit is around ₩19,000–30,000 before medicine. Keep the receipts for your travel insurance.

Do I need an appointment?

Not at a neighbourhood clinic. Walk in with your passport. International clinics at big hospitals usually want a booking.

Can I buy medicine without a prescription in Korea?

Yes. Pharmacies sell everyday medicine over the counter, and 24-hour convenience stores sell a short list of basics such as fever, pain and cold medicine.

What number do I call for an English interpreter?

1330, the Korea Travel Hotline: free, 7am to midnight, and it can join a three-way call. After midnight, try Danuri on 1577-1366.
